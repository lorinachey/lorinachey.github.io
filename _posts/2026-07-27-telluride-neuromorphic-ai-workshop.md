This summer I spent three weeks in the mountains of Telluride, Colorado for the [2026 Telluride Neuromorphic AI Workshop](https://sites.google.com/view/telluride-2026/home) — an intense, collaborative, and genuinely fun experience that I will remember for a long time.

## A workshop with deep roots

The Telluride workshop is one of the longest-running gatherings in neuromorphic engineering and neuro-inspired AI. It began in 1993 as a two-day meeting in Idyllwild, California, growing out of the labs of Carver Mead and Christof Koch at Caltech and Terry Sejnowski at the Salk Institute. Organized by Sejnowski, Rodney Douglas, and Koch with a seed grant from the National Science Foundation, that first gathering captured the early excitement around building electronic systems that mimic biological neural computation — work famously associated with pioneers like Misha Mahowald.

The following year, the workshop moved to Telluride, and it has been held there ever since. Continuously supported by the NSF for more than 25 years, it has brought together thousands of researchers for hands-on, project-based collaboration. The format is closer to a summer research lab than a conventional conference: morning lectures and tutorials, afternoon and evening project work, and plenty of opportunity to cross topic boundaries. In 2026, projects spanned areas including the neural basis of intention, neuromorphic robotics and integrated circuits, and spatiotemporal dynamics in neural computation — all organized around the idea of bringing the organizing principles of neural cognition into AI, and using AI to better understand how brains work.

## Three weeks in Telluride

The 2026 workshop ran from June 28 to July 17. Days were packed with lectures, demos, robotics experiments, and late-night project sessions in a mountain town that somehow manages to feel both intense and restorative. Working alongside researchers from neuroscience, robotics, hardware, and machine learning was a reminder of how much there is to learn when you leave your usual research bubble — and how much energy comes from building something together in a short stretch of time.

## Best Robot Trainer Award

At the closing ceremony, I was honored to receive the **Best Robot Trainer Award**. It was a fun and unexpected highlight of the workshop, and a great way to cap off weeks of hands-on robotics work with the Telluride community. The photo at the top of this post is from the award ceremony — still smiling about it!

## On the air with KOTO-FM

Another memorable part of the workshop was getting to participate in a show on [KOTO-FM](https://koto.org/), Telluride’s community radio station. Sharing the workshop with a local audience was a special way to connect the research happening in town with the people who call Telluride home.

## Looking ahead

I left Telluride with new collaborators, new ideas, and a deeper appreciation for the neuromorphic community’s blend of rigor, creativity, and play. If you get the chance to apply in a future year, I highly recommend it — the mountains, the projects, and the people make it unlike anything else in the research calendar.
